The optimization problem

The states and parameters to optimize are collected in one decision variable $\mathbf{x}$:

$$\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n} f(\mathbf{x}) \qquad\text{subject to}\quad h(\mathbf{x}) = 0\ \ (\text{equality}), \qquad g(\mathbf{x}) \le 0\ \ (\text{inequality})$$

$f$ is the objective to reduce; $h$ and $g$ are constraints that must hold; the points that satisfy them form the feasible region. A local minimum has no nearby feasible point with a smaller objective; a global minimum has no feasible point at all with a smaller objective. Whether a minimum exists and is unique are separate questions.

Camera calibration minimizes reprojection error over $K$, $\mathbf{d}$ and poses (ch. 3); the Kalman gain minimizes the corrected variance (ch. 4, 6); pose-graph SLAM minimizes weighted relative-pose residuals (ch. 6). This lecture is the general toolbox behind all of them — and behind MPC later in the course.

Direction and step size

Every iterative method picks a direction $\mathbf{d}_k$ and a step size $\alpha_k$, then updates:

$$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + \alpha_k\mathbf{d}_k, \qquad \varphi(\alpha) = f(\mathbf{x}_k + \alpha\mathbf{d}_k), \qquad \varphi'(0) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)^\T\mathbf{d}_k$$

If $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)^\T\mathbf{d}_k < 0$ the direction is a descent direction: for a small enough positive step the objective decreases. $\mathbf{d}$ need not be a unit vector. If the inner product is 0, first-order information cannot decide. A line search keeps the direction fixed while choosing $\alpha$; the direction is recomputed at the new point.

The quadratic local model

The gradient is the slope; the Hessian describes how the slope changes. With $f_k = f(\mathbf{x}_k)$, $\nabla f_k$ and $H_k = \nabla^2f(\mathbf{x}_k)$, for a displacement $\mathbf{s}$ (the actual step is $\mathbf{s} = \alpha\mathbf{d}$):

$$f(\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{s}) \approx f_k + \nabla f_k^\T\mathbf{s} + \tfrac12\mathbf{s}^\T H_k\mathbf{s}, \qquad m_k(\mathbf{s}) = f_k + \nabla f_k^\T\mathbf{s} + \tfrac12\mathbf{s}^\T B_k\mathbf{s}$$

$B_k$ is the curvature matrix used for the direction. With $B_k = H_k$ it is the Taylor model; other choices trade cost against stability — and an arbitrary $B$ need not be close to $H$.

Optimality conditions

At an interior point of a twice-differentiable $f$:

ConditionMeaning
Necessarylocal minimum $\Rightarrow\ \nabla f = 0,\ H \succeq 0$flat, and nowhere curving down
Sufficient$\nabla f = 0,\ H \succ 0 \Rightarrow$ strict local minimum$\mathbf{d}^\T H\mathbf{d} > 0$ for every $\mathbf{d} \ne 0$
Saddle$\nabla f = 0$, $H$ indefinitepositive and negative curvature coexist

A singular positive-semidefinite Hessian is inconclusive (higher-order terms decide).

$f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2$: $\nabla f = (3x^2 - 3,\ 2y) = 0$ at $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$; $H = \diag(6x, 2)$.

  • $(1, 0)$: $H = \diag(6, 2) \succ 0$ → strict local minimum ($f = -2$).
  • $(-1, 0)$: $H = \diag(-6, 2)$, indefinite → saddle point.

Not a global minimum either: $f \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$.

Convexity

$f$ is convex if it stays below every chord: $f((1-t)\mathbf{x} + t\mathbf{y}) \le (1 - t)f(\mathbf{x}) + tf(\mathbf{y})$ for $0 \le t \le 1$. For differentiable $f$ this is equivalent to lying above every tangent:

$$f(\mathbf{y}) \ge f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\T(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x}) \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^\star) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; f(\mathbf{y}) \ge f(\mathbf{x}^\star)\ \ \forall\mathbf{y}$$

Linear least squares is convex — one stationary point, found in one step. Problems with rotations (calibration, pose graphs) are not, so they can have several local minima: that is why chapter 3 and 6 insist on good initial values (linear estimates, odometry) before the nonlinear refinement.

Computing a direction from the model

Set the gradient of the quadratic model to zero:

$$\nabla_\mathbf{s}m_k(\mathbf{d}_k) = \nabla f_k + B_k\mathbf{d}_k = 0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \boxed{B_k\mathbf{d}_k = -\nabla f_k}$$

If $B_k \succ 0$ the model has a unique minimum and the direction is a descent direction:

$$\nabla f_k \ne 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla f_k^\T\mathbf{d}_k = -\mathbf{d}_k^\T B_k\mathbf{d}_k < 0$$

Gradient descent and line search

Taking the curvature matrix as the identity:

$$B_k = I \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{d}_k = -\nabla f_k, \qquad \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - \alpha_k\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k), \qquad \nabla f_k^\T\mathbf{d}_k = -\|\nabla f_k\|_2^2 < 0$$

It is a descent direction wherever $\nabla f_k \ne 0$ and very cheap, but it ignores how curvature differs between directions. When the differences are large it zigzags and slows down:

On a quadratic with condition number $\kappa = \lambda_\max/\lambda_\min$ (here $20/2 = 10$), exact-line-search gradient descent reduces the error by at worst a factor $\left(\frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}\right)^2 \approx 0.67$ per step. As $\kappa$ grows this factor approaches 1 — badly scaled problems are slow for first-order methods.

Backtracking with the Armijo condition

Hold the direction fixed and accept a step only if it achieves a fraction $c_1$ of the decrease the slope predicts (sufficient decrease):

$$f(\mathbf{x}_k + \alpha\mathbf{d}_k) \le f_k + c_1\,\alpha\,\nabla f_k^\T\mathbf{d}_k, \qquad \nabla f_k^\T\mathbf{d}_k < 0,\quad 0 < c_1 < 1,\quad 0 < \beta < 1$$

Try $\alpha \leftarrow 1$ first; while the test fails, shrink $\alpha \leftarrow \beta\alpha$. This sets the step for the current direction; it does not guarantee a global minimum.

$f(x) = x^2$ at $x_0 = 1$: $\nabla f = 2$, $d = -2$, $c_1 = 0.5$, $\beta = 0.5$.

  1. $\alpha = 1$: $f(1 - 2) = 1$; test value $1 + 0.5 \cdot 1 \cdot (2)(-2) = -1$. $1 \le -1$? No → shrink.
  2. $\alpha = 0.5$: $f(0) = 0$; test value $1 + 0.5 \cdot 0.5 \cdot (-4) = 0$. $0 \le 0$ ✓ → accept, $x_1 = 0$ (here exactly the minimum).

Newton's method

Use the actual Hessian so the direction accounts for curvature:

$$B_k = H_k = \nabla^2f(\mathbf{x}_k): \qquad H_k\mathbf{d}_k = -\nabla f_k, \qquad \nabla f_k^\T\mathbf{d}_k = -\mathbf{d}_k^\T H_k\mathbf{d}_k < 0\ \ \text{if}\ H_k \succ 0$$

The direction comes from an approximate model, so a line search still checks the actual decrease ($0 < \alpha_k \le 1$; near the solution $\alpha = 1$ is accepted and convergence is very fast). If $H_k$ is not positive definite, a corrected $B_k \succ 0$ is used instead (e.g. $H_k + \lambda I$).

$f = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 10(x_2 - 2)^2$ from $\mathbf{x}_0 = (0, 0)$: $\nabla f = (-2, -40)$, $H = \diag(2, 20)$.

  • Newton: $\mathbf{d} = -H^{-1}\nabla f = (1, 2)$ → $\mathbf{x}_1 = (1, 2)$, the exact minimum, in one step (a quadratic model of a quadratic is exact).
  • Gradient descent, exact line search: $\alpha = \frac{\nabla f^\T\nabla f}{\nabla f^\T H\nabla f} \approx 0.0501$ → $\mathbf{x}_1 \approx (0.100, 2.004)$: $x_2$ is nearly solved, $x_1$ barely moved — and the zigzag of the figure above follows.

For $f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$ (minima at $\pm1$, maximum at 0) start Newton at $x_0 = 0.4$, where $f'' = -2.08 < 0$. The iterates go $0.4 \to -0.246 \to 0.036 \to 0.000$ while $f$ rises from −0.29 to 0: pure Newton converged to the maximum. With $H \not\succ 0$ the Newton direction can point uphill — hence the line search and the positive-definite correction.

Gauss–Newton for least squares

In least-squares problems the objective is half the squared norm of a residual vector $\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{x})$ (all the errors), with Jacobian $J_{ij} = \partial r_i/\partial x_j$:

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac12\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_2^2, \qquad \mathbf{r}(\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{d}) \approx \mathbf{r}_k + J_k\mathbf{d}, \qquad \min_\mathbf{d}\tfrac12\|\mathbf{r}_k + J_k\mathbf{d}\|_2^2$$ $$J_k^\T(\mathbf{r}_k + J_k\mathbf{d}_k) = 0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \boxed{J_k^\T J_k\,\mathbf{d}_k = -J_k^\T\mathbf{r}_k}$$

If $J_k$ has full column rank, $J_k^\T J_k \succ 0$ and the direction is unique. Comparing with the true derivatives shows which term Gauss–Newton drops:

$$\nabla f = J^\T\mathbf{r}, \qquad \nabla^2f = \underbrace{J^\T J}_{\text{first derivatives}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^m r_i\nabla^2r_i}_{\text{needs second derivatives}}, \qquad B_k^\text{GN} = J_k^\T J_k$$
  • If $\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}$ (linear), $\nabla^2r_i = 0$: Gauss–Newton is exact and gives the normal equations $A^\T A\mathbf{x} = A^\T\mathbf{b}$.
  • If the residuals are small at the solution and their curvature moderate, the dropped term is small → near-Newton speed without second derivatives.
  • If $J^\T J$ is nearly singular, even a small approximation error can strongly affect the direction.

Find $x$ with $r(x) = x^2 - 2 = 0$: $J = 2x$, so $d = -r/J$ and $x \leftarrow x - \frac{x^2 - 2}{2x}$. From $x_0 = 1$: $1.5 \to 1.41667 \to 1.41422$ — √2 to five digits after three steps. (For a single residual with a square Jacobian, GN coincides with Newton's root-finding iteration.)

Levenberg–Marquardt

If the objective does not decrease enough, either shrink the step or recompute the direction. LM penalizes large steps:

$$\min_\mathbf{s}\ \tfrac12\|\mathbf{r}_k + J_k\mathbf{s}\|_2^2 + \frac{\eta_k}{2}\|\mathbf{s}\|_2^2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad (J_k^\T J_k + \eta_kI)\,\mathbf{d}_k = -J_k^\T\mathbf{r}_k$$

With $\eta > 0$ the matrix is positive definite even when $J$ is rank-deficient.

Accepting or rejecting a step

Compare the actual decrease with the decrease predicted by the Gauss–Newton model (without the damping term):

$$q_k(\mathbf{d}) = \tfrac12\|\mathbf{r}_k + J_k\mathbf{d}\|_2^2, \qquad a_k = f(\mathbf{x}_k) - f(\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{d}_k), \qquad p_k = q_k(0) - q_k(\mathbf{d}_k) > 0, \qquad \gamma_k = \frac{a_k}{p_k}$$
  • $\gamma_k \approx 1$: the model predicts well; $\gamma_k \le 0$: the objective did not decrease.
  • Accept if $\gamma_k > \tau$ ($0 < \tau < 1$): $\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{d}_k$; if the prediction was accurate, $\eta$ can be reduced next time.
  • Reject if $\gamma_k \le \tau$: stay, raise $\eta_k$, recompute $\mathbf{d}_k$, re-evaluate.

Typical numbers question: $f(\mathbf{x}_k) = 10$, predicted reduction $p_k = 4$, and the trial point gives $f = 7$: $\gamma = 3/4 = 0.75 > \tau$ → accept (and you may decrease $\eta$). If the trial point gives $f = 10.5$: $\gamma = -0.125$ → reject and increase $\eta$.

Constraints and feasible directions

With constraints, the direction must also respect them (to first order, at a feasible $\mathbf{x}$):

$$h(\mathbf{x} + \alpha\mathbf{d}) \approx h(\mathbf{x}) + \alpha J_h\mathbf{d} \;\Rightarrow\; J_h\mathbf{d} = 0$$

Move along the tangent of the constraint surface.

$$\nabla g_i(\mathbf{x})^\T\mathbf{d} \le 0$$

Move inward or tangentially; inactive inequalities ($g_i < 0$) do not restrict small steps.

With nonlinear constraints a straight step along the tangent satisfies the first-order condition but can still leave the surface — later methods must correct for that. (For affine constraints the linear form is exact.)

Lagrange multipliers and the KKT conditions

One equality constraint

At a constrained minimum, every tangent direction ($\nabla h^\T\mathbf{d} = 0$) must have zero rate of change ($\nabla f^\T\mathbf{d} = 0$) — otherwise moving along it, or opposite to it, would decrease $f$. So $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to the constraint's tangent, i.e. parallel to $\nabla h$:

$$\nabla f + \lambda\nabla h = 0, \qquad \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol\lambda) = f(\mathbf{x}) + \boldsymbol\lambda^\T h(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \text{several constraints: } \nabla f + J_h^\T\boldsymbol\lambda = 0$$

$\lambda$ relates the two gradients and is unrestricted in sign. This describes a regular local minimum: $\nabla h \ne 0$ (one constraint) or $J_h$ of full row rank (several).

Minimize $x^2 + y^2$ subject to $x + y = 1$: $2x + \lambda = 0$, $2y + \lambda = 0$, $x + y = 1$ → $x = y = 0.5$, $\lambda = -1$, $f^* = 0.5$.

Beyond the slides: $-\lambda$ is the sensitivity of the optimal cost to the constraint level. For $x + y = c$, $f^*(c) = c^2/2$ and $df^*/dc = c = 1 = -\lambda$.

One inequality constraint

On an active boundary $g(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ inward moves are allowed, which fixes the multiplier's sign:

$$\nabla g^\T\mathbf{d} \le 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla f^\T\mathbf{d} \ge 0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \nabla f + \mu\nabla g = 0,\quad \mu \ge 0 \qquad (-\nabla f = \mu\nabla g)$$

The objective "wants" to leave the feasible set (its descent direction $-\nabla f$ points outward, along $\nabla g$). Complementarity: if the constraint is slack, $g(\mathbf{x}) < 0 \Rightarrow \mu = 0$; in general $\mu\,g(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ ($\mu$ can also be 0 on the boundary).

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol\lambda, \boldsymbol\mu) = f(\mathbf{x}) + \boldsymbol\lambda^\T h(\mathbf{x}) + \boldsymbol\mu^\T g(\mathbf{x})$$
Stationarity$\nabla_\mathbf{x}\mathcal{L} = \nabla f + J_h^\T\boldsymbol\lambda + J_g^\T\boldsymbol\mu = 0$
Primal feasibility$h(\mathbf{x}) = 0,\ g(\mathbf{x}) \le 0$
Dual feasibility$\boldsymbol\mu \ge 0$
Complementarity$\mu_ig_i(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ for all $i$

Necessary: at a local minimum where LICQ holds (gradients of the equalities and active inequalities are linearly independent), KKT is satisfied. Sufficient: with convex $f$, $g_i$ and affine $h$, a KKT point is a global minimum. $\boldsymbol\lambda$ is sign-free; for nonconvex problems KKT alone does not guarantee a minimum.

Minimize $(x - 2)^2$ subject to $x \le 1$, i.e. $g(x) = x - 1 \le 0$.

  • Guess active ($x = 1$): stationarity $2(1 - 2) + \mu = 0 \Rightarrow \mu = 2 \ge 0$ ✓, complementarity $2 \cdot 0 = 0$ ✓ → KKT point, and since the problem is convex, the global minimum.
  • With $x \le 3$ instead: the unconstrained minimum $x = 2$ is feasible ($g = -1 < 0$), so $\mu = 0$ and $x^* = 2$.

Quadratic programming

A quadratic objective with linear constraints. With equalities only, the KKT conditions are linear, so one linear solve gives both the optimum and the multipliers:

$$\min_\mathbf{x}\ \tfrac12\mathbf{x}^\T Q\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{q}^\T\mathbf{x}\ \ \text{s.t.}\ A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}, \qquad Q = Q^\T,\ \nabla f = Q\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{q}$$ $$Q\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{q} + A^\T\boldsymbol\lambda = 0,\quad A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \begin{bmatrix} Q & A^\T \\ A & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x} \\ \boldsymbol\lambda \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\mathbf{q} \\ \mathbf{b} \end{bmatrix}$$

$Q \succeq 0$ makes it a convex QP; $Q \succ 0$ with $A$ of full row rank is sufficient for unique primal and dual solutions.

$Q = \diag(2, 4)$, $\mathbf{q} = (-2, -4)$, constraint $x_1 + x_2 = 1$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \lambda \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; x_1 = \tfrac13,\ x_2 = \tfrac23,\ \lambda = \tfrac43$$

Without the constraint the minimum would be $Q^{-1}(-\mathbf{q}) = (1, 1)$, which violates $x_1 + x_2 = 1$; the constraint pulls the solution onto the line, more strongly in the cheaper direction $x_1$ (smaller curvature).

With inequalities $G\mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{b}_g$ you must also decide which ones are active at the optimum: equalities always hold; for inequalities, check whether equality holds. Active-set methods estimate the active set, solve the equality QP, and revise the set using the multiplier signs and feasibility; interior-point methods also solve QPs.

Sequential quadratic programming

A general nonlinear problem is approximated by a QP around the current point:

$$\min_\mathbf{d}\ \nabla f_k^\T\mathbf{d} + \tfrac12\mathbf{d}^\T B_k\mathbf{d} \quad\text{s.t.}\quad h_k + J_{h,k}\mathbf{d} = 0, \quad g_k + J_{g,k}\mathbf{d} \le 0, \qquad B_k \approx \nabla^2_{\mathbf{xx}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_k, \boldsymbol\lambda_k, \boldsymbol\mu_k)$$
  • $B_k$ approximates the Hessian of the Lagrangian, so it reflects the curvature of both the objective and the constraints.
  • The current point may be infeasible, so the constant terms $h_k$, $g_k$ are kept.
  • In the basic form $B_k$ is corrected to be positive definite.

Line search with constraints: the merit function

A step can lower $f$ while violating the constraints more, so both are combined:

$$V(\mathbf{x}) = \|h(\mathbf{x})\|_1 + \sum_i\max(g_i(\mathbf{x}), 0), \qquad \psi_\rho(\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{x}) + \rho\,V(\mathbf{x}),\quad \rho > 0$$

$V$ adds how far each equality is from 0 and only the positive parts of the inequalities; $V = 0$ exactly when every constraint holds. Choose α so that ψ decreases enough, with ρ fixed during one line search. A decrease in ψ does not mean that every constraint holds or that a minimum has been reached.

When to stop

ConditionTest (tolerance ε)
stationarity$\|\nabla_\mathbf{x}\mathcal{L}\|_\infty \le \varepsilon$
primal feasibility$\|h(\mathbf{x})\|_\infty \le \varepsilon,\ \max_ig_i(\mathbf{x}) \le \varepsilon$
dual feasibility$\mu_i \ge -\varepsilon$ for all $i$
complementarity$\max_i|\mu_ig_i(\mathbf{x})| \le \varepsilon$

Even when the step or the objective change is small, the KKT conditions must be checked separately — a method can stall far from a solution. In nonconvex problems a small KKT error does not guarantee a minimum. Scale each tolerance to its condition; for unconstrained problems, check the gradient norm.

Practice questions

Distinguish a local from a global minimum. Why does convexity matter so much in practice? Name one nonconvex problem from this course.

A local minimum beats every nearby feasible point; a global minimum beats every feasible point. Iterative methods only use local information, so in general they find local minima. For a convex problem every local minimum is global (and a zero-gradient point is optimal), so any convergent method gives the right answer. Nonconvex examples: pose-graph optimization and camera calibration (rotations/angles enter through sines and cosines), which is why they need good initial values.

For $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$ at $(1, 1)$, is $\mathbf{d} = (1, -1)$ a descent direction? Is $\mathbf{d} = (1, 0)$?

$\nabla f = (2x, 6y) = (2, 6)$. For $(1, -1)$: $\nabla f^\T\mathbf{d} = 2 - 6 = -4 < 0$ → descent. For $(1, 0)$: $2 > 0$ → ascent (moving in $+x$ increases $f$ initially).

Find and classify all stationary points of $f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2$.

$\nabla f = (3x^2 - 3, 2y) = 0 \Rightarrow (\pm1, 0)$. $H = \diag(6x, 2)$. At $(1, 0)$: $\diag(6, 2) \succ 0$ → strict local minimum, $f = -2$. At $(-1, 0)$: $\diag(-6, 2)$, indefinite → saddle. The local minimum is not global ($f \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$), so the function is not convex.

Run backtracking with the Armijo condition on $f(x) = x^2$ from $x_0 = 1$ along $d = -f'(x_0)$, using $c_1 = 10^{-4}$ and $\beta = 0.5$. Which α is accepted? Why is α = 1 rejected even though it does not increase $f$?

$f' = 2$, $d = -2$, $\nabla f^\T d = -4$. α = 1: $x = -1$, $f = 1$; test $1 \le 1 + 10^{-4}(1)(-4) = 0.9996$? No. α = 0.5: $x = 0$, $f = 0 \le 1 - 0.0002$ ✓ → accept. α = 1 does not decrease $f$ at all ($1 \to 1$, it jumps across the valley); Armijo demands a decrease proportional to the step and the slope, which rules out such useless steps.

On $f = (x_1 - 1)^2 + 10(x_2 - 2)^2$ from $(0, 0)$, compare one Newton step with one exact-line-search gradient step. Explain the zigzag of gradient descent.

Newton: $\mathbf{d} = -\diag(2, 20)^{-1}(-2, -40) = (1, 2)$ → $(1, 2)$, the minimum, in one step. Gradient: $\nabla f = (-2, -40)$, $\alpha = \|\nabla f\|^2/(\nabla f^\T H\nabla f) \approx 0.0501$ → $(0.100, 2.004)$. The gradient is dominated by the steep $x_2$ direction (curvature 20 vs 2), so the step nearly solves $x_2$ and hardly moves $x_1$. With exact line search consecutive gradients are orthogonal, so the iterates zigzag across the narrow valley; the contraction per step is about $((\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1))^2 \approx 0.67$ with $\kappa = 10$.

Apply pure Newton ($\alpha = 1$) to $f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$ from $x_0 = 0.4$. Where does it converge, and what safeguard would have prevented this?

$f' = 4x^3 - 4x$, $f'' = 12x^2 - 4$. At 0.4: $f' = -1.344$, $f'' = -2.08 < 0$, $d = -f'/f'' = -0.646$ → $x_1 = -0.246$; then $0.036$, then $\approx 0$. Newton converged to $x = 0$, the local maximum, while $f$ rose from −0.29 to 0 — Newton solves $\nabla f = 0$ and does not care about the type of stationary point. Safeguards: check $\nabla f^\T\mathbf{d} < 0$ and replace $H$ by a positive-definite $B$ (e.g. $H + \lambda I$) when it is not, plus a line search that requires decrease (or use LM-style damping).

Apply Gauss–Newton to the single residual $r(x) = x^2 - 2$ from $x_0 = 1$ for three iterations.

$J = 2x$, $d = -(J^\T J)^{-1}J^\T r = -r/J$: $x_1 = 1 - (-1)/2 = 1.5$; $x_2 = 1.5 - 0.25/3 = 1.41667$; $x_3 = 1.41667 - 0.006944/2.8333 = 1.41422$. The error roughly squares each iteration (quadratic convergence for a zero-residual problem).

Which term of the Hessian does Gauss–Newton drop? When is the approximation exact, when is it good, and when is it poor?

$\nabla^2f = J^\T J + \sum_ir_i\nabla^2r_i$; GN keeps $J^\T J$ and drops $\sum r_i\nabla^2r_i$. Exact when the residuals are linear ($\nabla^2r_i = 0$, e.g. $A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}$). Good when the residuals are small near the solution (well-fitting model) or only mildly curved. Poor for large-residual problems with strongly curved residuals, or when $J^\T J$ is nearly singular — then GN can take huge or wrong steps, which LM's damping guards against.

An LM step has predicted reduction $p_k = 4$ with $f(\mathbf{x}_k) = 10$, and $\tau = 0.25$. Decide what to do if the trial point gives (a) $f = 7$, (b) $f = 9.5$, (c) $f = 10.5$.

(a) $\gamma = 3/4 = 0.75 > \tau$ → accept; the model is reliable, so $\eta$ may be decreased (towards Gauss–Newton). (b) $\gamma = 0.5/4 = 0.125 \le \tau$ → reject (insufficient agreement), increase $\eta$, recompute $\mathbf{d}$. (c) $\gamma = -0.5/4 = -0.125 < 0$ → the objective increased: reject and increase $\eta$, which shortens the step and turns it towards $-J^\T\mathbf{r}$.

Explain what happens to the LM direction as $\eta \to 0$ and as $\eta \to \infty$, and why this makes LM robust.

$\eta \to 0$: $(J^\T J)\mathbf{d} = -J^\T\mathbf{r}$, the Gauss–Newton step — fast near a solution. $\eta \to \infty$: $\mathbf{d} \approx -J^\T\mathbf{r}/\eta = -\nabla f/\eta$, a short gradient-descent step — guaranteed to decrease $f$ if short enough. By raising $\eta$ after a bad step and lowering it after a good one, LM blends the safety of gradient descent far from the solution with the speed of GN near it, and $J^\T J + \eta I \succ 0$ even if $J$ is rank-deficient.

Minimize $f = x^2 + y^2$ subject to $x + y = 1$ with a Lagrange multiplier. Check that $\nabla f$ is parallel to $\nabla h$ at the solution.

$\mathcal{L} = x^2 + y^2 + \lambda(x + y - 1)$: $2x + \lambda = 0$, $2y + \lambda = 0$, $x + y = 1$ → $x = y = 0.5$, $\lambda = -1$. At the solution $\nabla f = (1, 1)$ and $\nabla h = (1, 1)$: parallel ✓ ($\nabla f + \lambda\nabla h = 0$). The tangent direction $(1, -1)$ is orthogonal to $\nabla f$, so moving along the line cannot lower $f$ to first order.

For $\min(x - 2)^2$ s.t. $x \le 1$, someone proposes $x = 0$ with $\mu = 4$. Check all four KKT conditions. Then find the true KKT point.

$g = x - 1$. Stationarity: $2(0 - 2) + 4 = 0$ ✓. Primal feasibility: $0 \le 1$ ✓. Dual feasibility: $\mu = 4 \ge 0$ ✓. Complementarity: $\mu g = 4(0 - 1) = -4 \ne 0$ ✗ → not a KKT point (a positive multiplier is only allowed on an active constraint). True point: active, $x = 1$, $\mu = 2$ — all four hold, and by convexity it is the global minimum.

Solve the equality QP $\min\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^\T\diag(2, 4)\mathbf{x} - 2x_1 - 4x_2$ subject to $x_1 + x_2 = 1$ via the KKT system. Is the solution unique?

$2x_1 - 2 + \lambda = 0$, $4x_2 - 4 + \lambda = 0$, $x_1 + x_2 = 1$. From the first two, $x_1 = 1 - \lambda/2$, $x_2 = 1 - \lambda/4$; substituting, $2 - \tfrac34\lambda = 1 \Rightarrow \lambda = \tfrac43$, $x_1 = \tfrac13$, $x_2 = \tfrac23$. Unique: $Q = \diag(2, 4) \succ 0$ and $A = [1\ 1]$ has full row rank, so the KKT matrix is nonsingular.

Why does SQP need a merit function for its line search, and what is the role of ρ?

In constrained problems, a step can reduce $f$ while increasing constraint violation (or vice versa), so neither $f$ nor $V$ alone measures progress. The merit function $\psi_\rho = f + \rho V$ combines them; a step is accepted if it reduces ψ enough. ρ sets the price of violation: too small and the iterates may wander infeasibly; large enough (larger than the multipliers' magnitude, for the exact $\ell_1$ penalty) and minimizers of ψ coincide with feasible minimizers. A decrease in ψ alone still does not prove feasibility or optimality — the KKT tests do.

Pick a method (and say why) for: (a) camera intrinsic calibration; (b) a pose graph with loop closures; (c) linear-model MPC with box limits on steering and acceleration; (d) trajectory optimization with nonlinear vehicle dynamics and obstacle-avoidance constraints.

(a) and (b): nonlinear least squares → Gauss–Newton/LM (residuals and Jacobians are available; LM for robustness, often with sparse solvers and robust losses). (c) Quadratic cost, linear dynamics and linear inequality limits → a QP (active-set or interior-point), solved every control cycle. (d) Nonlinear objective and constraints → SQP (linearize dynamics and constraints into a QP each iteration, with a merit-function line search).

An optimizer stops because the step became smaller than $10^{-8}$. Why is that not enough to declare success, and what should be checked?

Tiny steps can also come from stalling: a bad scaling, an overly damped LM, a line search that keeps shrinking, or a point where progress is blocked by constraints. Success means the KKT conditions hold within tolerance: small stationarity residual $\|\nabla_\mathbf{x}\mathcal{L}\|_\infty$, satisfied constraints, $\mu \ge 0$ and small complementarity (for unconstrained problems, a small gradient norm). Even then, for a nonconvex problem it may be only a local minimum (or a saddle), so check curvature or try other starting points.
