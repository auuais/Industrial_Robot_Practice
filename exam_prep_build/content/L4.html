Wheel odometry: from encoder counts to a pose

An encoder counts $N$ ticks per wheel revolution ($N$ already includes the gear ratio and the counting method, e.g. ×4 quadrature). If the wheel rolls without slipping:

$$\Delta\phi = 2\pi\frac{\Delta c}{N}, \qquad \Delta s = r\,\Delta\phi$$

Example (slide 6): $r = 0.05$ m, $N = 1000$, $\Delta c = 1300 - 1200 = 100$ → $\Delta\phi = 0.62832$ rad (36°), $\Delta s = 31.416$ mm.

With wheel separation $b$, the two wheels travel on arcs $b/2$ outside and inside the body center:

$$\Delta s_R = \Delta s + \frac b2\Delta\theta, \qquad \Delta s_L = \Delta s - \frac b2\Delta\theta$$

Adding and subtracting:

$$\Delta s = \frac{\Delta s_R + \Delta s_L}{2}, \qquad \Delta\theta = \frac{\Delta s_R - \Delta s_L}{b}$$

The sum gives the travel, the difference gives the rotation (Δθ in rad).

Pose update. If the curvature is constant over a short step, the heading in the middle of the step approximates the travel direction:

$$\alpha = \theta_k + \frac{\Delta\theta}{2}, \qquad \Delta x \approx \Delta s\cos\alpha, \qquad \Delta y \approx \Delta s\sin\alpha, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} x_{k+1} \\ y_{k+1} \\ \theta_{k+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_k \\ y_k \\ \theta_k \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta y \\ \Delta\theta \end{bmatrix}$$

If $\Delta\theta = 0$, $\alpha = \theta_k$. For small $\Delta\theta$ the arc length is a good approximation of the straight-line displacement. (This is the midpoint idea of Lecture 2 applied to odometry.)

Initial pose $(0, 0, 0)$, $r = 0.05$ m, $b = 0.30$ m, $N = 1000$; left wheel +100 counts, right wheel +200 counts.

  1. $\Delta s_L = 31.416$ mm, $\Delta s_R = 62.832$ mm.
  2. $\Delta s = 47.124$ mm, $\Delta\theta = (62.832 - 31.416)/300 = 0.10472$ rad $= 6^\circ$.
  3. Mid heading $\alpha = 3^\circ$: $\Delta x \approx 47.059$ mm, $\Delta y \approx 2.466$ mm.
  4. New pose $(x, y, \theta) \approx (0.047059\ \mathrm{m},\ 0.002466\ \mathrm{m},\ 6^\circ)$.

For constant curvature the exact displacement is $\Delta x = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta\theta}[\sin(\theta_k + \Delta\theta) - \sin\theta_k]$, $\Delta y = -\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta\theta}[\cos(\theta_k + \Delta\theta) - \cos\theta_k]$. For the example it gives (47.038, 2.465) mm — 0.02 mm from the mid-heading result. The mid-heading rule uses the arc length instead of the chord, a negligible difference for small Δθ; that is why it is the standard choice.

Orientation as a quaternion and the ROS Odometry message

ROS stores orientation as a unit quaternion in the order $(q_x, q_y, q_z, q_w)$. A rotation by angle $\theta$ about the unit axis $\mathbf{u}$:

$$(q_x, q_y, q_z) = \mathbf{u}\sin\frac\theta2, \qquad q_w = \cos\frac\theta2, \qquad q_x^2 + q_y^2 + q_z^2 + q_w^2 = 1$$

No rotation is $q = (0, 0, 0, 1)$. In planar motion (roll = pitch = 0, $\mathbf{u} = (0, 0, 1)$, counter-clockwise from above positive) only $q_z$ and $q_w$ vary:

$$(q_x, q_y, q_z, q_w) = \left(0,\ 0,\ \sin\frac\theta2,\ \cos\frac\theta2\right)$$

When odometry lies: slip

Both wheels are commanded 0.5 rad/s for 2 s, so the expected travel is $\Delta s_\text{cmd} = r\,\omega_\text{cmd}\Delta t = 0.05$ m. Odometry only sees wheel rotation:

Drive conditionMeasured rotationActual motionOdometry estimate
driving without slip1 rad50 mm50 mm ✓
wheel blocked from turning0 rad0 mm0 mm ✓
wheel spinning in place1 rad0 mm50 mm ✗

The encoder cannot detect spinning (or sliding). Odometry is a model of motion that assumes rolling without slip; correcting it requires an independent position observation — which is what the Kalman filter combines.

The 1-D Kalman filter: models and assumptions

With wheel travel and position observations aligned in frame and time, start with one axis:

$$x_k = x_{k-1} + u_{k-1} + w_{k-1}$$

$x$ true position, $u$ travel computed from the wheels, $w$ travel error the model does not explain: $E[w] = 0$, $Q = E[w^2]$.

$$z_k = x_k + n_k$$

$z$ observed position, $n$ observation noise: $E[n] = 0$, $R = E[n^2]$. Initial error and all noises zero-mean and mutually uncorrelated.

Notation: $\hat x^-_k$ is the estimate before the update (prediction), $\hat x^+_k$ after it; the estimation error is $e = x - \hat x$ and, for a zero-mean error, its variance is $P = E[e^2]$.

Prediction: the variance grows

$$\hat x^-_k = \hat x^+_{k-1} + u_{k-1}, \qquad e^-_k = x_k - \hat x^-_k = e^+_{k-1} + w_{k-1}$$ $$P^-_k = E[(e^+_{k-1})^2] + 2E[e^+_{k-1}w_{k-1}] + E[w^2_{k-1}] = P^+_{k-1} + Q$$

The cross term vanishes because the previous error and the new travel error are uncorrelated. Each prediction adds the travel uncertainty $Q$.

Correction: deriving the Kalman gain

Take a weighted average of prediction and observation (step index dropped):

$$\hat x^+ = \hat x^- + K(z - \hat x^-) = (1 - K)\hat x^- + Kz$$

Substitute $z = x + n$: $e^+ = x - \hat x^+ = (1 - K)e^- - Kn$. The two errors are uncorrelated, so

$$P^+(K) = (1 - K)^2P^- + K^2R$$

This is a parabola in $K$. Set its derivative to zero ($P^- \ge 0$, $R > 0$):

$$\frac{dP^+}{dK} = -2(1 - K)P^- + 2KR = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; K^* = \frac{P^-}{P^- + R}, \qquad \frac{d^2P^+}{dK^2} = 2(P^- + R) > 0$$ $$P^+(K^*) = \frac{R^2P^- + (P^-)^2R}{(P^- + R)^2} = \frac{P^-R}{P^- + R} = (1 - K^*)P^-$$
  • Weighted average: $\hat x^+ = \dfrac{R\,\hat x^- + P^-z}{P^- + R}$ — each source is weighted by the other's variance, so the more certain one dominates.
  • Information adds: $\dfrac1{P^+} = \dfrac1{P^-} + \dfrac1R$, so $P^+ \le \min(P^-, R)$. Combining two independent estimates is always at least as good as the better one.

One full cycle, and what Q and R do

Previous estimate 8 m with $P^+ = 3$ m²; wheel travel 2 m with $Q = 1$ m²; observation 12 m with $R = 1$ m².

  1. Predict: $\hat x^- = 8 + 2 = 10$ m, $P^- = 3 + 1 = 4$ m².
  2. Gain: $K = 4/(4 + 1) = 0.8$.
  3. Innovation: $\nu = z - \hat x^- = 12 - 10 = 2$ m.
  4. Correct: $\hat x^+ = 10 + 0.8 \times 2 = 11.6$ m, $P^+ = (1 - 0.8) \times 4 = 0.8$ m² ($\sigma = 0.89$ m).

A larger $R$ favors the prediction; a larger $Q$ favors the observation. $Q$ and $R$ are the variances set in the filter — tuning knobs that should match reality: too small a $Q$ makes the filter overconfident in its model and slow to react.

When observations pause

Set the initial $\hat x^+_0$ and $P^+_0$; each step's corrected value and variance feed the next prediction. With no observation, the correction is skipped: $\hat x^+_k = \hat x^-_k$, $P^+_k = P^-_k$, so σ grows by $Q$ every step. The first new observation pulls it back down.

With constant $Q$ and $R$ the filter settles where predict and correct balance: $P^- = P^+ + Q$ and $P^+ = P^-R/(P^- + R)$ give $(P^-)^2 - QP^- - QR = 0$. For $Q = 0.09$, $R = 1$: $P^- = 0.348$, $P^+ = 0.258$ ($\sigma = 0.51$ m) and a constant gain $K = 0.258$ — the flat level in the lower plot.

Estimating velocity from position: the matrix filter

Now drop the wheel input and let a constant-velocity model predict the position from the previous velocity estimate, while only the position is observed:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} p \\ v \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x}_k = F\mathbf{x}_{k-1} + \mathbf{w}_{k-1}, \quad F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \Delta t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad z_k = H\mathbf{x}_k + n_k, \quad H = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

The covariance matrix

With $e_p = p - \hat p$ and $e_v = v - \hat v$:

$$P = E[\mathbf{e}\mathbf{e}^\T] = \begin{bmatrix} P_{pp} & P_{pv} \\ P_{pv} & P_{vv} \end{bmatrix}$$

Diagonal entries are the variances of each error; the off-diagonal cross-covariance says whether the two errors grow and shrink together. Even without process noise the prediction creates it, because a velocity error integrates into position:

$$e^-_{p,k} = e^+_{p,k-1} + \Delta t\,e^+_{v,k-1} \quad\Rightarrow\quad P^-_{pv,k} = P^+_{pv,k-1} + \Delta t\,P^+_{vv,k-1}$$

Prediction covariance and process noise

If the previous error and the new process noise are uncorrelated:

$$\mathbf{e}^-_k = F\mathbf{e}^+_{k-1} + \mathbf{w}_{k-1}, \qquad P^-_k = E[\mathbf{e}^-_k(\mathbf{e}^-_k)^\T] = FP^+_{k-1}F^\T + Q$$

Model the unknown disturbance as a constant acceleration error $a$ within the step ($E[a] = 0$, variance $\sigma_a^2$). It moves position by $a\Delta t^2/2$ and velocity by $a\Delta t$:

$$\mathbf{w} = G\,a, \quad G = \begin{bmatrix} \Delta t^2/2 \\ \Delta t \end{bmatrix}, \qquad Q = G\sigma_a^2G^\T = \sigma_a^2\begin{bmatrix} \Delta t^4/4 & \Delta t^3/2 \\ \Delta t^3/2 & \Delta t^2 \end{bmatrix}$$

The same acceleration error affects both states, so $Q$'s off-diagonal entries are not zero either.

Innovation and the gain matrix

$$\nu = z - H\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-, \qquad S = E[\nu^2] = HP^-H^\T + R$$

With one position observation, $S$ is a positive scalar. Minimizing the corrected variance of the $i$-th state, $P^+_{ii}(K_i) = P^-_{ii} - 2K_i(P^-H^\T)_i + K_i^2S$, gives

$$K_i = \frac{(P^-H^\T)_i}{S}, \qquad K = \frac{P^-H^\T}{S} = \begin{bmatrix} P^-_{pp}/S \\ P^-_{pv}/S \end{bmatrix}$$

The position gain uses the position variance; the velocity gain uses the cross-covariance.

$\hat{\mathbf{x}}^+_0 = [0\ \mathrm{m}, 1\ \mathrm{m/s}]^\T$, $P^+_0 = \diag(1, 1)$, $\Delta t = 1$ s, $\sigma_a^2 = 0.1$ m²/s⁴; then $z = 1.5$ m with $R = 0.25$ m².

  1. $\hat{\mathbf{x}}^- = F\hat{\mathbf{x}}^+_0 = [1, 1]^\T$, $Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0.025 & 0.05 \\ 0.05 & 0.1 \end{bmatrix}$, $P^- = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + Q = \begin{bmatrix} 2.025 & 1.05 \\ 1.05 & 1.1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  2. $\nu = 1.5 - 1 = 0.5$ m, $S = 2.025 + 0.25 = 2.275$ m².
  3. $K_p = 2.025/2.275 \approx 0.8901$, $K_v = 1.05/2.275 \approx 0.4615$ s⁻¹.
  4. Corrections $+0.445$ m and $+0.231$ m/s: $\hat{\mathbf{x}}^+ \approx [1.445\ \mathrm{m},\ 1.231\ \mathrm{m/s}]^\T$.
  5. $P^+ = P^- - KHP^- \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.223 & 0.115 \\ 0.115 & 0.615 \end{bmatrix}$.

The robot is further ahead than predicted, and because position and velocity errors are positively correlated, it probably also moved faster than assumed — so the velocity is raised too. If $P_{pv} = 0$, a position observation gives no velocity correction. The filter learns velocity only through the correlation the motion model builds up.

Bias: confident but wrong

Suppose every observation reads 12 m while the robot sits at 10 m: $z = x + b + n$ with a constant bias $b = 2$ m that the filter does not model (here $n = 0$, $P_0 = 4$ m², $Q = 0$, $R = 0.04$ m²).

AfterEstimateFilter σActual error
1 observation11.980 m0.199 m1.98 m
40 observations12.000 m0.032 m2.00 m

The variance can shrink while the bias remains: $P$ measures only the uncertainty the model knows about. Even with the bias in the state, observing only $z = p + b$ cannot separate the two values — every point of the line $p + b = 12$ fits. Repeating the same kind of observation never helps; an independent observation (e.g. another sensor reporting $p = 10$ m) identifies state A.

Expect a "why did the filter go wrong although σ was small?" question. Answer pattern: the error source was not in the model (bias, slip, wrong $Q/R$, wrong frame or time), so the covariance cannot reflect it; fix the model or add an independent measurement that makes the error observable.

Practice questions

Why does a ROS robot keep both an odom frame and a map frame? Which one would you use for a local obstacle-avoidance controller, and which for navigating to a building room?

odom is continuous and smooth (it integrates wheel motion without jumps) but drifts without bound; map is globally accurate (corrected by localization) but the robot's pose in it can jump when a correction arrives. A local controller wants smooth relative motion over a few seconds → odom. Reaching a room requires global accuracy → map. Keeping both, linked by map → odom, gives each consumer what it needs.

A wheel of radius 0.08 m has an encoder giving 4096 counts per wheel revolution (after the gearbox and quadrature). How far does the wheel roll for 1024 counts?

$\Delta\phi = 2\pi \times 1024/4096 = \pi/2$ rad, $\Delta s = 0.08 \times \pi/2 \approx 0.1257$ m.

Same robot as slide 9 ($r = 0.05$ m, $b = 0.30$ m, $N = 1000$, start at $(0, 0, 0)$). The left wheel turns +300 counts, the right +100. Compute the new pose with the mid-heading rule.

$\Delta s_L = 94.248$ mm, $\Delta s_R = 31.416$ mm. $\Delta s = 62.832$ mm, $\Delta\theta = (31.416 - 94.248)/300 = -0.20944$ rad $= -12^\circ$ (a right turn, since the left wheel went further). $\alpha = -6^\circ$: $\Delta x = 62.832\cos6^\circ \approx 62.488$ mm, $\Delta y = -62.832\sin6^\circ \approx -6.568$ mm. Pose ≈ $(0.06249\ \mathrm{m}, -0.00657\ \mathrm{m}, -12^\circ)$.

Write the ROS quaternion for planar headings of 120° and −90°. Which heading does $q = (0, 0, 0.2588, 0.9659)$ represent?

120°: $(0, 0, \sin60^\circ, \cos60^\circ) = (0, 0, 0.8660, 0.5)$. −90°: $(0, 0, \sin(-45^\circ), \cos(-45^\circ)) = (0, 0, -0.7071, 0.7071)$. For $(0, 0, 0.2588, 0.9659)$: $\theta = 2\operatorname{atan2}(0.2588, 0.9659) = 2 \times 15^\circ = 30^\circ$.

In a nav_msgs/Odometry message, in which frame is the pose expressed and in which the twist? Why is it sensible to express the twist that way for a wheeled robot?

The pose is the pose of child_frame_id (base_link) expressed in header.frame_id (odom). The twist is expressed in the child frame, base_link. For a wheeled robot the natural velocities are body-fixed — forward speed and yaw rate (a differential drive cannot move sideways, so the lateral body velocity is ~0); in odom the same motion would mix into $\dot x$ and $\dot y$ depending on the heading.

For each case of slide 14 say what the odometry error is and how an extra sensor could reveal it: (a) wheel spinning on ice; (b) robot pushed sideways by a person while the wheels are stopped.

(a) The encoder counts rotation, so odometry reports 50 mm of travel while the robot stays put — a 50 mm error that grows as long as it spins. An IMU (no acceleration), visual odometry or LiDAR scan matching would show no motion, and motor current/command-vs-motion checks can flag it. (b) The wheels do not turn, so odometry reports no motion while the robot moved — undetectable by the encoders; an IMU would sense the push, and any absolute observation (GNSS, landmarks, scan matching) would show the displacement. In both cases the fix is an independent position observation fused by a filter.

Derive the optimal gain for $\hat x^+ = \hat x^- + K(z - \hat x^-)$ with $z = x + n$, and show that $P^+ \le \min(P^-, R)$.

$e^+ = x - \hat x^+ = (1 - K)e^- - Kn$. With $e^-$ and $n$ uncorrelated and zero-mean, $P^+ = (1 - K)^2P^- + K^2R$. $dP^+/dK = -2(1 - K)P^- + 2KR = 0 \Rightarrow K^* = P^-/(P^- + R)$; the second derivative $2(P^- + R) > 0$ confirms a minimum. Substituting, $P^+ = P^-R/(P^- + R)$. Since $R/(P^- + R) \le 1$, $P^+ \le P^-$; since $P^-/(P^- + R) \le 1$, $P^+ \le R$. Equivalently $1/P^+ = 1/P^- + 1/R$.

One cycle: $\hat x^+_{k-1} = 5$ m, $P^+_{k-1} = 2$ m², wheel travel $u = 1$ m with $Q = 0.5$ m², observation $z = 6.5$ m with $R = 0.5$ m². Compute $\hat x^-$, $P^-$, $K$, $\hat x^+$, $P^+$.

$\hat x^- = 6$ m, $P^- = 2.5$ m²; $K = 2.5/3 \approx 0.833$; $\nu = 0.5$ m; $\hat x^+ = 6 + 0.833 \times 0.5 \approx 6.417$ m; $P^+ = (1 - 0.833) \times 2.5 \approx 0.417$ m² (σ ≈ 0.65 m), below both 2.5 and 0.5. ✓

Describe what the 1-D filter does in these limits: (a) $R \to 0$; (b) $R \to \infty$; (c) $P^- \to 0$; (d) $Q = 0$ and many observations.

(a) $K \to 1$: the estimate jumps to the observation and $P^+ \to 0$ (perfect sensor). (b) $K \to 0$: observations are ignored; the filter becomes pure dead reckoning with $P$ growing by $Q$ each step. (c) $K \to 0$: the prediction is already certain, so nothing changes. (d) $P$ keeps shrinking roughly like $R/n$ and $K \to 0$: the filter becomes ever more confident and ever slower to react — dangerous if the model is slightly wrong (a reason never to set $Q$ exactly to zero).

With $Q = 0.09$ m² and $R = 1$ m² every step, find the steady-state $P^-$, $P^+$, σ and gain. Compare with the lower plot of slide 21.

At steady state $P^- = P^+ + Q$ and $P^+ = P^-R/(P^- + R)$. Substituting gives $(P^-)^2 - QP^- - QR = 0$, so $P^- = \tfrac12(Q + \sqrt{Q^2 + 4QR}) = \tfrac12(0.09 + 0.6067) = 0.348$ m². Then $P^+ = 0.348/1.348 = 0.258$ m², σ ≈ 0.51 m, and $K = P^-/(P^- + R) \approx 0.258$. The slide's σ curve sits at about 0.5 m outside the gap — consistent.

For the constant-velocity model with $\Delta t = 0.5$ s and $\sigma_a^2 = 0.2$ m²/s⁴, compute $F$ and $Q$. Why are $Q$'s off-diagonal terms positive?

$$F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad Q = 0.2\begin{bmatrix} 0.015625 & 0.0625 \\ 0.0625 & 0.25 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.003125 & 0.0125 \\ 0.0125 & 0.05 \end{bmatrix}$$

One random acceleration $a$ causes both $\Delta p = a\Delta t^2/2$ and $\Delta v = a\Delta t$ with the same sign, so the two process-noise components are perfectly positively correlated.

In the position–velocity example the velocity was corrected from 1 to 1.231 m/s although only position was measured. Explain the mechanism. What would happen if the initial covariance were $\diag(1, 1)$ and the update happened before any prediction?

The velocity gain is $K_v = P^-_{pv}/S$. The prediction $p_k = p_{k-1} + \Delta t\,v_{k-1}$ makes position errors depend on velocity errors, creating $P^-_{pv} = 1.05 > 0$; a position innovation therefore implies a velocity error of the same sign. If the update came first with $P = \diag(1, 1)$, then $P_{pv} = 0$, so $K_v = 0$: the position would be corrected but the velocity would not change at all.

A GNSS receiver has an unknown constant offset of 2 m. After a minute, the Kalman filter reports σ = 3 cm but the robot is 2 m off. Why is σ so small? Would adding the bias to the state fix it? What would?

$P$ only describes errors the model contains (zero-mean, uncorrelated noise); every consistent observation shrinks it, so σ → small while the unmodeled bias passes straight into the estimate. Adding $b$ to the state alone does not help: with only $z = p + b$ the pair is unobservable (a line of solutions). It helps if another, independent measurement constrains $p$ or $b$ — e.g. wheel odometry over a known start, a surveyed landmark, a second GNSS antenna/base station — or if the bias is calibrated beforehand.

During an 8–11 s observation gap (slide 21), what happens to the estimate and to σ? Which parameter controls how fast σ grows?

The filter runs prediction only: $\hat x^+ = \hat x^-$ follows the motion model (dead reckoning), and $P$ increases by $Q$ every step, so σ grows (like $\sqrt{P_0 + nQ}$). $Q$ controls the growth. At the first new observation $K$ is larger than usual (because $P^-$ is larger), so the estimate moves strongly toward it and σ drops back.

After the square drive of slide 3, a localization system publishes the map → odom correction (−20°, (−0.26, 0.41) m). A teammate says the robot's whole logged trajectory is now correct. Is it?

No. The correction is a single rigid transform applied to all odom points; it makes the current pose (and the last segment) right, but the shape distortion of the earlier path — caused by errors that accumulated at different times — remains (slide 4: "aligning the current pose does not remove the error in the past path"). Correcting each past pose differently requires smoothing or pose-graph optimization over the whole trajectory with loop closures (chapter 6).
