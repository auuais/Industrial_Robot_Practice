States, inputs and parameters

The whole course describes a system through three kinds of quantities. For the vehicle of this lecture:

KindSymbolMeaning
State$\mathbf{x} = [X, Y, \psi, v]^\T$position, yaw angle, velocity — what the dynamics change
Input$\mathbf{u} = [\delta, a]^\T$steering angle and acceleration — what we command
Parameters$\boldsymbol\theta = [L, \dots]^\T$vehicle specs such as the wheelbase — constant
$$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = f_\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k; \boldsymbol\theta, h)$$

The discrete model $f_\mathrm{d}$ computes the state $h$ seconds later from the current state and input. To build it you need three kinds of information (slide 3): the structure and motion conditions (which links connect through which joint axes; the vehicle layout and the assumption of no lateral wheel slip), the model parameters (link lengths, wheelbase, sensor mounting pose, joint-angle zero offsets) and measurement data (joint angles, speed, actual steering angle — each with its measurement time). Derive the equations from the structure, then substitute the specs and measurements.

Cartesian, cylindrical and spherical coordinates

With the same origin and axes, a point can be written in three ways. Range sensors such as LiDAR naturally measure the spherical form.

Cylindrical $(r, \alpha, z)$ — $r$ is the distance from the origin to the projection of P on the $xy$-plane:

$$x = r\cos\alpha, \qquad y = r\sin\alpha$$

Spherical $(\rho, \alpha, \varphi)$ — $\rho$ distance to the point, $\alpha$ azimuth from $+x$, $\varphi$ angle from $+z$:

$$x = \rho\sin\varphi\cos\alpha,\quad y = \rho\sin\varphi\sin\alpha,\quad z = \rho\cos\varphi$$

With the elevation $\varepsilon$ measured from the $xy$-plane: $\varepsilon = 90^\circ - \varphi$, so $z = \rho\sin\varepsilon$ and $r = \rho\cos\varepsilon$.

Point at 2 m, azimuth 30°, 60° from $+z$ (slide 6):

$$\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\sin60^\circ\cos30^\circ \\ 2\sin60^\circ\sin30^\circ \\ 2\cos60^\circ \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 1.5000 \\ 0.8660 \\ 1.0000 \end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{m}$$

Check: $1.5^2 + 0.866^2 + 1^2 = 4 = \rho^2$. ✓

Sensor datasheets differ: some give the angle from the $+z$ axis ($\varphi$), others the elevation from the horizontal plane ($\varepsilon$). Mixing them swaps $\sin$ and $\cos$ — always check which angle you were given.

Frames, poses and rotation matrices

Three frames appear everywhere: the World frame G fixed to the workspace, the Body frame B that moves and rotates with the robot, and the Sensor frame S in which a sensor reports its measurements.

The pose of frame B seen from G is the pair $(R_\mathrm{GB}, \mathbf{t}_\mathrm{GB})$:

  • $R_\mathrm{GB}$ — the directions of B's axes as seen from G;
  • $\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{GB}$ — the position of B's origin, expressed in G.
$$R_\mathrm{GB} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\psi & -\sin\psi \\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi \end{bmatrix}$$

Each column is a unit axis of B written in G: the first column is $x_\mathrm{B}$, the second is $y_\mathrm{B}$.

With a common origin a point transforms as $\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{G} = R_\mathrm{GB}\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B}$. Rotation matrices are orthonormal, so

$$R_\mathrm{GB}^\T R_\mathrm{GB} = I, \qquad R_\mathrm{BG} = R_\mathrm{GB}^{-1} = R_\mathrm{GB}^\T$$

Adding the translation gives the general rule $\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{G} = R_\mathrm{GB}\,\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B} + \mathbf{t}_\mathrm{GB}$ — both terms on the right are vectors expressed in G.

Read $R_\mathrm{GB}$ as "from B to G": it takes coordinates written in B and returns them written in G. Chains must have matching inner subscripts — $R_\mathrm{GB}R_\mathrm{BE} = R_\mathrm{GE}$ is valid, $R_\mathrm{GB}R_\mathrm{EB}$ is a bug. The same rule checks every product of transforms in this course (and Lecture 3 uses exactly this $T_{AB}$ convention).

Homogeneous transforms: chain, invert, and points vs displacements

Rotation and translation fit into one matrix if a point gets an extra coordinate equal to 1 (3D shown; in 2D the matrix is 3×3):

$$T_\mathrm{AB} = \begin{bmatrix} R_\mathrm{AB} & \mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB} \\ \mathbf{0}_{1\times3} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_\mathrm{B} = \begin{bmatrix} x_\mathrm{B} \\ y_\mathrm{B} \\ z_\mathrm{B} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_\mathrm{A} = T_\mathrm{AB}\,\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_\mathrm{B}$$

Subtract the origin, then multiply by $R^\T$ from the left:

$$\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B} = R_\mathrm{AB}^\T\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{A} - R_\mathrm{AB}^\T\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB}$$ $$T_\mathrm{BA} = T_\mathrm{AB}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R_\mathrm{AB}^\T & -R_\mathrm{AB}^\T\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB} \\ \mathbf{0} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Chain frames by multiplying in order:

$$T_\mathrm{AC} = T_\mathrm{AB}\,T_\mathrm{BC}$$ $$R_\mathrm{AC} = R_\mathrm{AB}R_\mathrm{BC}, \quad \mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AC} = R_\mathrm{AB}\,\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{BC} + \mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB}$$

The inverse translation is not $-\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB}$. The vector $\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB}$ is written in frame A; the inverse needs B's view of A's origin, so it must also be rotated: $-R_\mathrm{AB}^\T\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB}$. Negating only works when $R = I$.

Position vectors and displacement vectors

If two points P and Q are transformed into A and subtracted, the translation cancels:

$$\Delta\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{A} = (R_\mathrm{AB}\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B,Q} + \mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB}) - (R_\mathrm{AB}\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B,P} + \mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB}) = R_\mathrm{AB}\,\Delta\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B}$$

A displacement (or velocity, or force) has no location, so only the rotation applies. Homogeneous coordinates handle this automatically: a position gets a last entry 1, a displacement gets 0, which multiplies the translation column by zero.

$$\tilde{\mathbf{p}} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \ \text{(position)}, \qquad \widetilde{\Delta\mathbf{p}} = \begin{bmatrix} \Delta\mathbf{p} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \ \text{(displacement)}$$

The Tool frame E has its origin at $(0.4, 0.2, 0.3)$ m in the Base frame B and is rotated 90° about $z_\mathrm{B}$:

$$T_\mathrm{BE} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 & 0.4 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & 0.2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0.3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
  1. Point $(0.1, 0, 0.05)$ in E: $T_\mathrm{BE}[0.1, 0, 0.05, 1]^\T = [0.4, 0.3, 0.35, 1]^\T$ — rotate to $(0, 0.1, 0.05)$, then add $(0.4, 0.2, 0.3)$.
  2. Displacement $0.1$ m along $+x_\mathrm{E}$: $\Delta\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B} = R_\mathrm{BE}[0.1, 0, 0]^\T = [0, 0.1, 0]^\T$ m — moving along the tool's $x$-axis means moving along the base's $y$-axis.
  3. Chain to the World: the Base origin is at $(1, 2, 0)$ in G, rotated 90° about $z_\mathrm{G}$. Then $R_\mathrm{GE} = R_\mathrm{GB}R_\mathrm{BE} = R_z(180^\circ)$ and $\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{GE} = R_\mathrm{GB}[0.4, 0.2, 0.3]^\T + [1, 2, 0]^\T = [-0.2, 0.4, 0.3]^\T + [1, 2, 0]^\T = [0.8, 2.4, 0.3]^\T$ m.

Forward kinematics of a 2-link planar arm

Each link contributes a rotation by its joint angle followed by a translation of its length along the new $x$-axis:

$$T_{i-1,i} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos q_i & -\sin q_i & L_i\cos q_i \\ \sin q_i & \cos q_i & L_i\sin q_i \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Chain them: $T_{02} = T_{01}T_{12}$, i.e. $R_{02} = R_{01}R_{12}$ and $\mathbf{t}_{02} = R_{01}\mathbf{t}_{12} + \mathbf{t}_{01}$.

In words: transform link 2's displacement into the Base frame and add it to the end of link 1.

Multiplying out and using $\cos q_1\cos q_2 - \sin q_1\sin q_2 = \cos(q_1+q_2)$:

$$\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B,E} = \begin{bmatrix} L_1\cos q_1 + L_2\cos(q_1+q_2) \\ L_1\sin q_1 + L_2\sin(q_1+q_2) \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \varphi_\mathrm{E} = q_1 + q_2$$

$L_1 = 0.4$ m, $L_2 = 0.3$ m, $q_1 = 30^\circ$, $q_2 = 60^\circ$:

$$x = 0.4\cos30^\circ + 0.3\cos90^\circ \approx 0.3464\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad y = 0.4\sin30^\circ + 0.3\sin90^\circ = 0.5000\ \mathrm{m}$$ $$T_\mathrm{BE} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0.3464 \\ 1 & 0 & 0.5000 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad T_\mathrm{GE} = T_\mathrm{GB}T_\mathrm{BE} \approx \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0.5000 \\ 0 & -1 & 2.3464 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

with the Base at $(1, 2)$ in G rotated 90°: $\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{GE} = R_\mathrm{GB}[0.3464, 0.5]^\T + [1, 2]^\T = [-0.5 + 1,\ 0.3464 + 2]^\T$.

Error sensitivity. If the model uses a second link 0.01 m longer than reality, with the angles fixed the computed end-effector shifts along link 2: $\Delta\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B,E} = [\cos(q_1+q_2),\ \sin(q_1+q_2)]^\T\Delta L_2$. For $q_1 + q_2 = 90^\circ$ that is $[0, 0.01]^\T$ m — parameter errors map directly into position errors.

Jacobian. Differentiating $\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B,E}$ gives how joint errors (or joint velocities) map to the tool:

$$J(\mathbf{q}) = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1\sin q_1 - L_2\sin(q_1+q_2) & -L_2\sin(q_1+q_2) \\ L_1\cos q_1 + L_2\cos(q_1+q_2) & L_2\cos(q_1+q_2) \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Delta\mathbf{p} \approx J\,\Delta\mathbf{q}$$

At $(30^\circ, 60^\circ)$: $J = \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 & -0.3 \\ 0.3464 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. A 0.5° error in each joint moves the tool by about $(-6.98, 3.02)$ mm. $\det J = L_1L_2\sin q_2$ vanishes when the arm is stretched or folded ($q_2 = 0$ or $180^\circ$): a singularity.

Inverse kinematics (find $\mathbf{q}$ for a target $(x, y)$) by the law of cosines:

$$\cos q_2 = \frac{x^2 + y^2 - L_1^2 - L_2^2}{2L_1L_2}, \qquad q_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x) - \operatorname{atan2}(L_2\sin q_2,\ L_1 + L_2\cos q_2)$$

Two solutions exist ($\pm q_2$, elbow up/down); none if $|\cos q_2| > 1$ (outside the workspace annulus $|L_1 - L_2| \le r \le L_1 + L_2$).

Sensors on a moving arm: transform chains and timestamps

Sensor S1 is mounted on the Tool (constant mounting transform $T_\mathrm{ES1}$); sensor S2 is fixed to the Base ($T_\mathrm{BS2}$). Only the arm part depends on the joint angles:

$$T_\mathrm{BS1}(\mathbf{q}) = T_\mathrm{BE}(\mathbf{q})\,T_\mathrm{ES1}, \qquad T_\mathrm{S2S1}(\mathbf{q}) = T_\mathrm{BS2}^{-1}\,T_\mathrm{BE}(\mathbf{q})\,T_\mathrm{ES1}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_\mathrm{S2} = T_\mathrm{S2S1}(\mathbf{q})\,\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_\mathrm{S1}$$

Evaluate right to left — start with the matrix nearest the vector — and use the mounting data and the joint angles at measurement time.

S1 sits 0.1 m along $+x_\mathrm{E}$; S2 is at $(0.1, 0.2)$ in B; axes of S1/E and of S2/B are parallel; joints at 30° and 60°.

  1. $\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{BS1} = R_\mathrm{BE}[0.1, 0]^\T + \mathbf{t}_\mathrm{BE} = [0, 0.1]^\T + [0.3464, 0.5]^\T = [0.3464, 0.6]^\T$, and $R_\mathrm{BS1} = R_\mathrm{BE}$ (90°).
  2. $T_\mathrm{S2S1} = T_\mathrm{BS2}^{-1}T_\mathrm{BS1}$: rotation stays 90°, translation $[0.3464 - 0.1,\ 0.6 - 0.2]^\T = [0.2464, 0.4]^\T$ (no rotation in $T_\mathrm{BS2}$, so the inverse just subtracts).
  3. S1 measures P at $(0.2, 0)$: $\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{S2} = R_{90}[0.2, 0]^\T + [0.2464, 0.4]^\T = [0, 0.2]^\T + [0.2464, 0.4]^\T = [0.2464, 0.6]^\T$ m.

Timestamps matter

$\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_\mathrm{B}(t_k) = T_\mathrm{BE}(\mathbf{q}(t_k))\,T_\mathrm{ES1}\,\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_\mathrm{S1}(t_k)$ — joint angles from an earlier time describe a different arm pose. In the slide example the sensor and point lie along link 2 (total 0.3 + 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.6 m):

Joint-angle time$q_1, q_2$Point in B [m]

$\Delta\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B} \approx [0.017438, -0.003236]^\T$ m, $\|\Delta\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B}\|_2 \approx 17.74$ mm — from a 50 ms mismatch alone.

A quick estimate: position error ≈ (distance from the joint) × (joint speed) × (time error). The point is 0.6 m from joint 2, whose absolute angle $q_1 + q_2$ changed by 1.5° (0.026 rad) in 50 ms → about 16 mm; joint 1 adds a little more. Fast motion, long lever arms and latency all multiply.

The kinematic bicycle model

Each left–right wheel pair is merged into one equivalent wheel. $R$ is the rear-axle center, $F$ the front-axle center, $L$ the wheelbase (distance between them) and $\delta$ the steering angle of the equivalent front wheel.

No lateral slip at R: the velocity there points along the wheel.

$$\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{R}^\mathrm{B} = \begin{bmatrix} v_\mathrm{R} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \iff -\sin\psi\,\dot X_\mathrm{R} + \cos\psi\,\dot Y_\mathrm{R} = 0$$

ICR geometry (turning radius $R_\mathrm{R} > 0$):

$$\tan\delta = \frac{L}{R_\mathrm{R}}, \qquad \dot\psi = \frac{v_\mathrm{R}}{R_\mathrm{R}}$$

Curvature (signed: left +, right −; unit 1/m):

$$\kappa_\mathrm{R} = \frac{\tan\delta}{L}, \qquad \dot\psi = v_\mathrm{R}\,\kappa_\mathrm{R}, \qquad R_\mathrm{R} = \frac{1}{|\kappa_\mathrm{R}|}$$

At the same curvature the yaw rate is proportional to speed.

The rear-axle velocity is rotated (not translated — it is a vector, like a displacement) into the World frame:

$$\begin{bmatrix} \dot X_\mathrm{R} \\ \dot Y_\mathrm{R} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\psi & -\sin\psi \\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_\mathrm{R} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \ \Rightarrow\ \dot X_\mathrm{R} = v_\mathrm{R}\cos\psi,\quad \dot Y_\mathrm{R} = v_\mathrm{R}\sin\psi$$
$$\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{R} = \begin{bmatrix} X_\mathrm{R} \\ Y_\mathrm{R} \\ \psi \\ v_\mathrm{R} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} \delta \\ a_\mathrm{R} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \dot{\mathbf{x}}_\mathrm{R} = \begin{bmatrix} v_\mathrm{R}\cos\psi \\ v_\mathrm{R}\sin\psi \\ \dfrac{v_\mathrm{R}}{L}\tan\delta \\ a_\mathrm{R} \end{bmatrix} = f_\mathrm{c}(\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{R}, \mathbf{u}; L), \quad a_\mathrm{R} = \frac{\mathrm{d}v_\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{d}t}$$

Example (slide 35). $v_\mathrm{R} = 5$ m/s, $\delta = 10^\circ$, $L = 2.5$ m: $\omega = \dot\psi = \frac{5}{2.5}\tan10^\circ \approx 0.352654$ rad/s, $\kappa_\mathrm{R} \approx 0.0705$ 1/m, $R_\mathrm{R} \approx 14.18$ m. With constant inputs the yaw rate is constant and the car drives a circle.

  • $\psi$ is measured from the World $X$-axis to the vehicle's forward direction; $v_\mathrm{R} > 0$ forward, $< 0$ reversing.
  • Use $\tan\delta$, not $\delta$ — they only agree for small angles — and radians in code.
  • "No lateral slip" is an assumption about the tires. It is what makes this model kinematic; section 2.12 shows where it breaks.

Rear axle or center of gravity? The slip angle β

On a rotating rigid body, speed and direction of the velocity depend on the reference point. The CG lies $l_\mathrm{r}$ ahead of the rear axle ($L = l_\mathrm{r} + l_\mathrm{f}$, $\mathbf{r}_\mathrm{RC}^\mathrm{B} = [l_\mathrm{r}, 0]^\T$):

  1. Position: $\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{G,C} = \mathbf{p}_\mathrm{G,R} + R_\mathrm{GB}[l_\mathrm{r}, 0]^\T$.
  2. Differentiate, including the rotation term: $\dot{\mathbf{p}}_\mathrm{G,C} = v_\mathrm{R}[\cos\psi, \sin\psi]^\T + l_\mathrm{r}\dot\psi[-\sin\psi, \cos\psi]^\T$.
  3. Back to the body frame: $\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{C}^\mathrm{B} = R_\mathrm{GB}^\T\dot{\mathbf{p}}_\mathrm{G,C} = [v_\mathrm{R},\ l_\mathrm{r}\dot\psi]^\T$ — the CG also moves sideways.
$$\tan\beta = \frac{l_\mathrm{r}\dot\psi}{v_\mathrm{R}} = \frac{l_\mathrm{r}}{L}\tan\delta \ \Rightarrow\ \beta = \arctan\!\left(\frac{l_\mathrm{r}}{L}\tan\delta\right), \qquad v_\mathrm{R} = v_\mathrm{C}\cos\beta, \qquad \dot\psi = \frac{v_\mathrm{C}\cos\beta}{L}\tan\delta$$

The CG travels in direction $\psi + \beta$, not $\psi$. Example (slide 39): $L = 2.5$ m, $l_\mathrm{r} = 1.25$ m, $\delta = 10^\circ$, $v_\mathrm{R} = 5$ m/s → $\beta = 5.0384^\circ$, $v_\mathrm{C} = 5.0194$ m/s, $\dot\psi = 0.352654$ rad/s (the body yaw rate is the same at every point).

Many papers (e.g. Kong et al. 2015, cited on slide 60) write the same model about the CG: $\dot X = v\cos(\psi+\beta)$, $\dot Y = v\sin(\psi+\beta)$, $\dot\psi = \frac{v}{l_\mathrm{r}}\sin\beta$. It is equivalent — from $\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{C}^\mathrm{B}$ above, $v_\mathrm{C}\sin\beta = l_\mathrm{r}\dot\psi$. Choosing the rear axle as reference simply makes $\beta$ disappear from the equations.

From continuous to discrete time: Euler, midpoint, RK4

To predict the next state, integrate the rate of change over one step $h = t_{k+1} - t_k$. The input is held constant over the step, but the state still changes inside it:

$$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + \int_{t_k}^{t_k+h} f_\mathrm{c}(\mathbf{x}(\tau), \mathbf{u}_k; L)\,\mathrm{d}\tau \;=\; f_\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k; L, h)$$

The integral is rarely solvable in closed form, so we approximate it by sampling the rate of change:

MethodRates evaluatedUpdateRHS evals
Forward Euler$\mathbf{k}_1 = f_\mathrm{c}(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k)$ — rate at the start, assumed constant$\mathbf{x}_k + h\mathbf{k}_1$1
Midpoint (RK2)$\mathbf{k}_1$; then $\mathbf{k}_2 = f_\mathrm{c}(\mathbf{x}_k + \tfrac{h}{2}\mathbf{k}_1, \mathbf{u}_k)$ at the estimated midpoint$\mathbf{x}_k + h\mathbf{k}_2$2
RK4$\mathbf{k}_1$ start · $\mathbf{k}_2 = f_\mathrm{c}(\mathbf{x}_k + \tfrac{h}{2}\mathbf{k}_1)$ · $\mathbf{k}_3 = f_\mathrm{c}(\mathbf{x}_k + \tfrac{h}{2}\mathbf{k}_2)$ · $\mathbf{k}_4 = f_\mathrm{c}(\mathbf{x}_k + h\mathbf{k}_3)$$\mathbf{x}_k + \tfrac{h}{6}(\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4)$4

$\mathbf{k}_2$ and $\mathbf{k}_3$ are both at the midpoint time but use different state estimates. RK4's weights 1-2-2-1 are Simpson's rule.

Initial state $[0, 0, 0, 5]^\T$ (m, m, rad, m/s), $\delta = 10^\circ$, $a = 0$, $L = 2.5$ m.

  1. Euler: $\mathbf{k}_1 = [5\cos0,\ 5\sin0,\ \tfrac{5}{2.5}\tan10^\circ,\ 0]^\T \approx [5, 0, 0.352654, 0]^\T$, so $\mathbf{x}_1 = \mathbf{x}_0 + 0.1\mathbf{k}_1 \approx [0.500000, 0, 0.035265, 5]^\T$. The car moved straight: $Y_1 = 0$.
  2. Midpoint: $\mathbf{x}_{1/2} = \mathbf{x}_0 + 0.05\mathbf{k}_1 \approx [0.25, 0, 0.017633, 5]^\T$. At that heading, $k_{2,X} = 5\cos(0.0176327) \approx 4.999223$ and $k_{2,Y} = 5\sin(0.0176327) \approx 0.088159$ m/s, so $\mathbf{x}_1 \approx [0.499922, 0.008816, 0.035265, 5]^\T$.
  3. RK4 ($Y$ only): $Y_1 = \tfrac{0.1}{6}(0 + 2\cdot0.088159 + 2\cdot0.088159 + 0.176290) \approx 0.008815$ m.
  4. Exact: $Y(0.1) = \tfrac{v}{\omega}(1 - \cos0.0352654) \approx 0.0088154$ m. Euler misses the curvature completely in the first step.

With constant speed and steering the exact solution is a circle ($\psi = \omega t$):

$$X_\mathrm{R}(t) = \int_0^t v_\mathrm{R}\cos(\omega\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau = \frac{v_\mathrm{R}}{\omega}\sin(\omega t), \qquad Y_\mathrm{R}(t) = \frac{v_\mathrm{R}}{\omega}\bigl(1 - \cos(\omega t)\bigr)$$

For small $h$ the error behaves like a power of the step size:

$$E(h) \approx C\,h^p \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{E(h/2)}{E(h)} \approx 2^{-p}, \qquad p \approx \log_2\frac{E(h)}{E(h/2)}$$

Euler: $0.162068 / 0.324140 \approx 0.50 \Rightarrow p = 1$. Midpoint: $0.000238 / 0.000953 \approx 0.25 \Rightarrow p = 2$. RK4: ratio $\approx 1/16 \Rightarrow p = 4$. This holds only when $h$ is small and the scheme is stable.

Cost is counted in evaluations of the right-hand side (RHS) $f_\mathrm{c}$. A 4 s horizon with $h = 0.1$ s takes $N = H/h = 40$ steps: Euler 40, midpoint 80, RK4 160 evaluations. Real run time also depends on how expensive $f_\mathrm{c}$ is and on the implementation.

Spend 40 evaluations over 4 s each way: Euler with $h = 0.1$ s ends 0.32 m off; RK4 with $h = 0.4$ s (10 steps × 4) ends about 2.5 µm off. Higher order wins by orders of magnitude whenever the solution is smooth — which is why RK4 is the default for simulation.

Numerical stability: the steering-actuator example

The real steering angle lags the command. A first-order response model with time constant $\tau$:

$$\tau\,\dot\delta_\mathrm{actual} + \delta_\mathrm{actual} = \delta_\mathrm{cmd}$$

After a step in the command, the response covers about 63.2 % ($1 - e^{-1}$) of the change in one $\tau$. With $\tau = 0.4$ s and a step from 0° to 10° at $t = 0.5$ s, $\delta_\mathrm{actual}(0.9) \approx 6.32^\circ$. Record the command and the actual angle separately, each with its own timestamp.

For a constant command, write the error $e = \delta_\mathrm{actual} - \delta_\mathrm{cmd}$, so $\dot e = -e/\tau$. One Euler step multiplies the error by an amplification factor:

$$e_{k+1} = e_k + h\left(-\frac{e_k}{\tau}\right) = \left(1 - \frac{h}{\tau}\right)e_k, \qquad \left|1 - \frac{h}{\tau}\right| < 1 \;\Rightarrow\; 0 < h < 2\tau$$

Stability is necessary, not sufficient: with $h = 0.6$ s the solution converges, but it overshoots and oscillates — something a first-order lag never does. Fast dynamics (small $\tau$) force small steps on explicit methods even when the rest of the model is slow.

Such problems are called stiff. The implicit (backward) Euler step $e_{k+1} = e_k - \frac{h}{\tau}e_{k+1}$ gives $e_{k+1} = e_k/(1 + h/\tau)$, which decays for any $h > 0$, at the price of solving an equation each step.

Where the kinematic model stops working, and how to validate a model

The normal acceleration is the component perpendicular to the path tangent: $a_\mathrm{n} = v_\mathrm{C}^2|\kappa_\mathrm{C}| = v_\mathrm{C}^2 / R_\mathrm{C}$. At $R_\mathrm{C} = 20$ m it is 1.25 m/s² at 5 m/s but 11.25 m/s² at 15 m/s — three times the speed, nine times the acceleration.

Tires need slip angles to produce that lateral force, which violates the no-slip assumption. Polack et al. (cited on slide 54) used the criterion

$$a_y = \frac{v_\mathrm{C}^2}{R_\mathrm{C}} \le 0.5\,\mu g$$

for their vehicle and conditions ($\mu$ road friction coefficient, $g$ gravity). With $\mu = 1$ the limit is 4.9 m/s², so on a 20 m radius the kinematic model is trustworthy only up to $v \approx \sqrt{4.9 \cdot 20} \approx 9.9$ m/s. Beyond that, a dynamic model with tire forces is needed (Week 12).

Validating with recorded driving data

Start from the measured initial state, feed the recorded steering and acceleration in time order, and compare $j$ steps ahead:

$$e_{k,j} = \bigl\|\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{meas},k+j} - \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{k+j|k}\bigr\|_2$$

where $k$ is the step where the prediction starts. Check that positions are compared at the same reference point and frame, at the same times and from the same initial state, and record the settings (parameters, step, data interval, horizon). In real-time use the future inputs must be assumed or planned instead of recorded.

Practice questions

What do the columns of $R_\mathrm{GB}$ represent, and why is $R_\mathrm{GB}^{-1} = R_\mathrm{GB}^\T$?

Column $i$ is the unit vector of B's $i$-th axis written in G coordinates (first column $x_\mathrm{B}$, second $y_\mathrm{B}$). Because these axes are unit length and mutually perpendicular, $R^\T R$ is the matrix of their dot products: 1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere, i.e. $R^\T R = I$, so $R^\T$ is the inverse. Geometrically, $R^\T$ undoes the rotation, giving $R_\mathrm{BG}$.

In 2D, $T_\mathrm{AB}$ has a rotation of 30° and translation $\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB} = (2, 1)$. Write $T_\mathrm{BA}$ numerically, and say what the common mistake would give.

$$R^\T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8660 & 0.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.8660 \end{bmatrix},\quad -R^\T\mathbf{t} = -\begin{bmatrix} 0.866\cdot2 + 0.5\cdot1 \\ -0.5\cdot2 + 0.866\cdot1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2.2321 \\ 0.1340 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$T_\mathrm{BA} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8660 & 0.5 & -2.2321 \\ -0.5 & 0.8660 & 0.1340 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The common mistake is to use $-\mathbf{t} = (-2, -1)$ as the translation, which ignores that $\mathbf{t}_\mathrm{AB}$ is expressed in A. Check: $T_\mathrm{AB}T_\mathrm{BA} = I$.

Using $T_\mathrm{BE}$ of slide 15 (90° about $z$, origin at $(0.4, 0.2, 0.3)$), express the tool's velocity $\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{E} = (0.2, 0, 0)$ m/s in B. Why is the translation not added?

$\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{B} = R_\mathrm{BE}\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{E} = (0, 0.2, 0)$ m/s. A velocity is a free vector like a displacement: it has a direction and magnitude but no location, so moving the origin does not change it. In homogeneous form it carries a 0 as its last entry, which cancels the translation column.

A LiDAR reports range 10 m, azimuth 45° and elevation 10°. Find the Cartesian point in the sensor frame.

With elevation, the horizontal distance is $\rho\cos\varepsilon = 10\cos10^\circ = 9.848$ m. Then $x = 9.848\cos45^\circ \approx 6.964$ m, $y \approx 6.964$ m, $z = \rho\sin\varepsilon = 10\sin10^\circ \approx 1.736$ m. (Using the polar-angle formula with $\varphi = 10^\circ$ by mistake would put the point almost straight up.)

For the 2-link arm with $L_1 = 0.4$ m, $L_2 = 0.3$ m, compute the end-effector position and orientation for $q_1 = 90^\circ$, $q_2 = -90^\circ$. Sketch to check.

$x = 0.4\cos90^\circ + 0.3\cos0^\circ = 0.3$ m, $y = 0.4\sin90^\circ + 0.3\sin0^\circ = 0.4$ m, $\varphi_\mathrm{E} = q_1 + q_2 = 0^\circ$. Sketch: link 1 points straight up to $(0, 0.4)$, link 2 turns 90° right and points along $+x$ to $(0.3, 0.4)$, with the tool facing $+x$. ✓

Inverse kinematics: find all joint-angle pairs that place the same arm's tool at $(0.3464, 0.5)$ m. Why are there two?

$$\cos q_2 = \frac{0.3464^2 + 0.5^2 - 0.4^2 - 0.3^2}{2\cdot0.4\cdot0.3} = \frac{0.37 - 0.25}{0.24} = 0.5 \ \Rightarrow\ q_2 = \pm60^\circ$$

For $q_2 = +60^\circ$: $q_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(0.5, 0.3464) - \operatorname{atan2}(0.3\sin60^\circ, 0.4 + 0.3\cos60^\circ) = 55.30^\circ - 25.29^\circ = 30^\circ$ (the slide's configuration). For $q_2 = -60^\circ$: $q_1 = 55.30^\circ + 25.29^\circ \approx 80.57^\circ$. The two solutions are mirror images across the line from the base to the target ("elbow down / elbow up"); both triangles with sides $L_1$, $L_2$ and the base–target distance fit.

In the slide 25 setup (joints 30°/60°), sensor S1 detects a point at $(0, 0.1)$ m in its own frame. Give its coordinates in B and in S2.

$\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_\mathrm{B} = T_\mathrm{BS1}[0, 0.1, 1]^\T$ with $T_\mathrm{BS1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0.3464 \\ 1 & 0 & 0.6 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$: $\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{B} = (-0.1 + 0.3464,\ 0 + 0.6) = (0.2464, 0.6)$ m. Since S2 is only translated by $(0.1, 0.2)$ from B: $\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{S2} = (0.1464, 0.4)$ m.

A camera on a robot arm sees a marker 0.8 m from the base joint while that joint rotates at 30°/s. The joint angles used for the transform are 50 ms old. Estimate the error and propose two fixes.

Error ≈ $r\,\omega\,\Delta t = 0.8 \times 0.5236 \times 0.05 \approx 0.021$ m ≈ 21 mm, in the direction of motion. Fixes: (1) interpolate the joint angles to the image timestamp (requires both streams on one clock with known latency); (2) hardware-trigger the camera from the controller, or reduce the latency; also, measure and compensate any fixed time offset (Lecture 3, temporal calibration).

A car with wheelbase 2.7 m drives at 10 m/s with $\delta = 5^\circ$. Find curvature, turning radius and yaw rate.

$\kappa = \tan5^\circ / 2.7 = 0.08749 / 2.7 \approx 0.0324$ 1/m, $R = 1/\kappa \approx 30.9$ m, $\dot\psi = v\kappa \approx 0.324$ rad/s ≈ 18.6°/s.

Same car, CG 1.2 m ahead of the rear axle, rear-axle speed 10 m/s. Compute β and the CG speed. In which direction does the CG move?

$\tan\beta = \frac{1.2}{2.7}\tan5^\circ = 0.4444 \times 0.08749 = 0.03888 \Rightarrow \beta \approx 2.23^\circ$. $v_\mathrm{C} = v_\mathrm{R}/\cos\beta \approx 10.008$ m/s. The CG moves at angle $\psi + \beta$, i.e. 2.23° to the left of the body axis (inside the turn), because the body's rotation adds a lateral component $l_\mathrm{r}\dot\psi = 0.389$ m/s at the CG.

Starting from $[X, Y, \psi, v] = [0, 0, 0, 10]$, $\delta = 5^\circ$, $a = 0$, $L = 2.7$ m, do one step of $h = 0.2$ s with Euler and with the midpoint method.

$\dot\psi = 0.32403$ rad/s. Euler: $\mathbf{x}_1 = [2.0,\ 0,\ 0.064806,\ 10]$. Midpoint: $\psi_{1/2} = 0.1 \times 0.32403 = 0.032403$; rates there: $\dot X = 10\cos0.032403 = 9.99475$, $\dot Y = 10\sin0.032403 = 0.32397$; so $\mathbf{x}_1 = [1.99895,\ 0.064795,\ 0.064806,\ 10]$. The midpoint result already "bends" left by 6.5 cm; Euler does not.

A simulator's position error after 10 s is 0.08 m with $h = 0.1$ s and 0.02 m with $h = 0.05$ s. What is the order of the method? Predict the error at $h = 0.025$ s.

Ratio $0.02/0.08 = 1/4 = 2^{-2}$, so $p = 2$ (a second-order method such as midpoint). Halving again divides by 4: about 0.005 m. This assumes the step is already small enough to be in the asymptotic regime.

For a 10 s simulation, compare Euler with $h = 0.01$ s against RK4 with $h = 0.1$ s in cost (RHS evaluations) and in expected accuracy.

Euler: 1000 steps × 1 = 1000 evaluations. RK4: 100 steps × 4 = 400 evaluations. Accuracy: Euler's error scales with $h$, RK4's with $h^4$; for the lecture's vehicle example, Euler at 0.01 s would still be off by roughly 3 cm after 4 s (scaling the 0.05 s value), while RK4 at 0.1 s is off by about $10^{-8}$ m. RK4 is cheaper and far more accurate here; Euler only competes when the RHS is extremely expensive or the solution is not smooth.

A steering actuator has $\tau = 0.25$ s. What is the largest stable Euler step? With $h = 0.3$ s and an initial error of −10°, list the errors for three steps and comment.

Stable for $0 < h < 2\tau = 0.5$ s. With $h = 0.3$ s the factor is $1 - 0.3/0.25 = -0.2$: errors −10° → +2° → −0.4° → +0.08°. It converges, but the sign flips every step — an overshoot the real first-order lag never shows. For a qualitatively correct (monotone) response Euler needs $h < \tau$ (factor between 0 and 1).

Using the criterion $a_y \le 0.5\mu g$, up to what speed can the kinematic model be used on a curve of radius 50 m when $\mu = 0.8$? Why does the model fail beyond that?

$v_\mathrm{max} = \sqrt{0.5 \times 0.8 \times 9.81 \times 50} \approx 14.0$ m/s ≈ 50 km/h. Beyond it the tires must generate large lateral forces, which requires lateral slip (slip angles); the model's core assumption — velocity along each wheel — no longer holds, so the predicted yaw rate and path become wrong. A dynamic bicycle model with tire forces is then required.

Why does model validation feed the recorded inputs into the model, and why does the prediction error in Kong et al.'s table grow with the horizon?

Recorded inputs isolate the model's error: if planned or guessed inputs were used, input errors would be mixed into the comparison. The error grows with the horizon because small heading errors integrate into lateral position errors that keep growing (roughly in proportion to distance), and model mismatch accumulates step after step; that is also why the standard deviation grows.
