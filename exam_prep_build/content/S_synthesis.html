One pattern behind the whole course

Almost every computation in Lectures 2–7 has the same anatomy: a model predicts what a calibrated sensor should see, the difference is a residual, residuals are weighted by their uncertainty, and an estimate is found by making the weighted residuals small — either recursively, one measurement at a time, or in a batch over a whole data set.

Combine the prediction (a "measurement" of $\mathbf{x}$ with covariance $P^-$) and the new observation into one cost:

$$J(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac12(\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}^-)^\T(P^-)^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}^-) + \tfrac12(\mathbf{z} - H\mathbf{x})^\T R^{-1}(\mathbf{z} - H\mathbf{x})$$

Setting $\nabla J = 0$ gives $\left((P^-)^{-1} + H^\T R^{-1}H\right)\hat{\mathbf{x}}^+ = (P^-)^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^- + H^\T R^{-1}\mathbf{z}$, i.e.

$$P^+ = \left((P^-)^{-1} + H^\T R^{-1}H\right)^{-1}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}}^+ = \hat{\mathbf{x}}^- + P^+H^\T R^{-1}(\mathbf{z} - H\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-)$$

which equals $K = P^-H^\T S^{-1}$ (matrix inversion lemma). In 1-D this is exactly $1/P^+ = 1/P^- + 1/R$ from chapter 4. With a nonlinear $h$, one Gauss–Newton step on $J$ from $\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-$ is the EKF update; iterating it gives the iterated EKF. So the filter of chapters 4 and 6 and the optimizers of chapters 3, 6 and 7 are two ways of solving the same kind of problem.

Mistakes that cost marks — in every lecture

ThemeChecklist
Frames$T_{AB}$ maps B coordinates into A; chains need matching inner indices; the inverse translation is $-R^\T\mathbf{t}$; velocities and displacements are only rotated; $\mathbf{t}_{GB}$ is expressed in G.
Anglesradians in code; wrap every angle difference into $[-\pi, \pi)$; quaternions use the half angle and ROS order $(x, y, z, w)$; $\tan\delta \ne \delta$; atan2, not atan.
Unitsmm vs m (a 1000× error); pixels vs normalized coordinates; variance (m²) vs standard deviation (m) — $Q$, $R$, $P$ are variances; pattern size in the real unit.
Timeuse joint angles / poses at the measurement time; temporal offsets and rolling shutter only show while moving; skip the update (not the prediction) when an observation is missing.
Uncertainty$P$ only covers modeled errors (bias!); stereo depth error grows with $Z^2$; information adds ($1/P^+ = 1/P^- + 1/R$); a confident filter can still be wrong.
Validationresidual ≠ accuracy ≠ repeatability; evaluate on data not used for fitting, with calibration parameters fixed; a small step is not convergence — check KKT/gradient.
  • $R^\T R = I$ and $\det R = +1$; $|q| = 1$; the last row of a homogeneous transform is $[0\ \cdots\ 0\ 1]$.
  • In 1-D: $0 \le K \le 1$ and $P^+ \le \min(P^-, R)$; the corrected value lies between prediction and observation.
  • Projected points need $Z_C > 0$; rectified disparity should be positive; $Z = fB/d$ shrinks as $d$ grows.
  • Euler is stable for $0 < h < 2\tau$ on a first-order lag; halving $h$ should cut the error by $2^p$.
  • Gauss–Newton/LM: the cost should decrease after an accepted step; a descent direction has $\nabla f^\T\mathbf{d} < 0$.

Mixed exam-style questions

These combine two or more lectures, the way longer exam problems often do.

A camera is mounted on the tool of the 2-link arm of Lecture 2 and detects a marker at pixel $(u, v)$ with known depth $Z_C$. Write the chain that gives the marker's World coordinates, and name the lecture/calibration that provides each factor.

Back-project with the intrinsics: $\mathbf{p}_C = Z_C\,K^{-1}[u, v, 1]^\T$ (after undistorting $(u, v)$) — camera intrinsics, Lecture 3. Then

$$\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_G = T_{GB}\,T_{BE}(\mathbf{q}(t))\,T_{EC}\,\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_C$$

$T_{GB}$: where the robot base is in the world (setup / localization); $T_{BE}(\mathbf{q})$: forward kinematics with link lengths (Lecture 2); $T_{EC}$: the camera's mounting pose on the tool (extrinsic calibration, hand–eye, Lecture 3); $\mathbf{q}(t)$ must be the joint angles at the image timestamp (temporal calibration, Lecture 3; timestamp error example, Lecture 2).

Use the kinematic bicycle model (Lecture 2) as the EKF motion model (Lecture 6): with Euler step $h$, write $f$ and the Jacobian $F$ for $\mathbf{x} = [X, Y, \psi, v]^\T$, $\mathbf{u} = [\delta, a]^\T$. Evaluate $F$ at $\mathbf{x} = (0, 0, 0, 5)$, $\delta = 10^\circ$, $L = 2.5$ m, $h = 0.1$ s.

$$f = \begin{bmatrix} X + hv\cos\psi \\ Y + hv\sin\psi \\ \psi + h\frac{v}{L}\tan\delta \\ v + ha \end{bmatrix}, \qquad F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -hv\sin\psi & h\cos\psi \\ 0 & 1 & hv\cos\psi & h\sin\psi \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \frac{h}{L}\tan\delta \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0.1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0.00705 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Speed errors grow position error along the heading and heading error (through $\tan\delta/L$); heading errors grow lateral position error (0.5 m per rad per step here). A Jacobian with respect to $\mathbf{u}$ would be needed if input noise is mapped into $Q$.

A stereo camera ($f = 800$ px, $B = 0.12$ m, $\sigma_d = 0.5$ px) measures the distance to an obstacle and feeds it to a 1-D Kalman filter with prior variance $P^- = 0.04$ m². Compute $R$ and the gain for an obstacle at 2 m and at 8 m. What does this mean for filter design?

$\sigma_Z = Z^2\sigma_d/(fB)$: 2 m → 0.0208 m, $R = 4.3 \times 10^{-4}$ m², $K = 0.04/(0.04 + 0.00043) \approx 0.99$. 8 m → 0.333 m, $R = 0.111$ m², $K = 0.04/0.151 \approx 0.26$. The measurement noise is distance-dependent, so $R$ must be recomputed for every measurement (from Lecture 3's error propagation); a constant $R$ would trust far measurements far too much (or near ones too little).

Explain with the language of Lecture 7 why a camera–IMU time offset $\Delta t$ and a position offset $b$ cannot be separated from constant-velocity data (Lecture 3, $y = v(\tau + \Delta t) + b$).

Treat $(\Delta t, b)$ as unknowns of a least-squares problem with residuals $r_i = y_i - v(\tau_i)(\tau_i + \Delta t) - b$. The Jacobian columns are $\partial r_i/\partial\Delta t = -v(\tau_i)$ and $\partial r_i/\partial b = -1$. With constant $v$ the first column is $v$ times the second, so $J$ loses rank, $J^\T J$ is singular and the Gauss–Newton system has no unique solution (a valley of equal cost along $v\Delta t + b = $ const). Varying the velocity makes the columns independent — the algebraic meaning of "exciting motion".

Compare how the EKF (Lecture 6) and the pose graph (Lecture 6, solved with Lecture 7's Gauss–Newton) handle a loop closure detected when the robot returns to its start.

An EKF keeps only the current pose (and its covariance): the loop-closure observation corrects the current pose, but the past poses — and the map points computed from them — stay as they were (the map → odom situation of Lecture 4). The pose graph keeps all poses as unknowns and minimizes all weighted residuals together; the loop closure's residual is spread over every odometry edge, correcting the entire trajectory, after which the map is rebuilt from the stored readings. The price is solving a (sparse) nonlinear least-squares problem instead of a constant-size update.

In Zhang's refinement the rotations $R_i$ must stay rotation matrices. Describe two ways to handle this with the methods of Lecture 7.

(1) Constrained optimization: keep the 9 entries as variables with equality constraints $h(R) = R^\T R - I = 0$ (and $\det R = 1$) — a nonlinear constrained problem for SQP with a merit function. (2) Unconstrained parametrization (what practical calibrators do): describe each rotation by 3 parameters (e.g. a rotation vector, or a quaternion that is renormalized), so every parameter value is a valid rotation; then Gauss–Newton/LM apply directly. Lecture 3's linear initialization also projects its estimate onto SO(3) for the same reason.

An EKF predicts a steering actuator with time constant $\tau = 0.05$ s using Euler steps of the filter period $h = 0.1$ s (Lecture 2 meets Lecture 6). What goes wrong and how can it be fixed?

Euler on a first-order lag is stable only for $h < 2\tau = 0.1$ s; at $h = 0.1$ s the amplification factor is $1 - h/\tau = -1$, so the predicted steering angle oscillates without decaying, corrupting the predicted yaw rate and position. Fixes: sub-step the prediction (several small Euler or RK4 steps per filter period), use the exact discrete solution $e_{k+1} = e^{-h/\tau}e_k$, use implicit Euler, or — if the actuator is much faster than the filter rate — model the steering as reaching the command instantly.

Show that the 1-D Kalman update is the minimizer of $J(x) = \frac{(x - \hat x^-)^2}{2P^-} + \frac{(z - x)^2}{2R}$ and that $1/P^+ = 1/P^- + 1/R$.

Set the derivative to zero:

$$J'(x) = \frac{x - \hat x^-}{P^-} - \frac{z - x}{R} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x\left(\frac{1}{P^-} + \frac{1}{R}\right) = \frac{\hat x^-}{P^-} + \frac{z}{R}$$ $$\hat x^+ = \frac{R\,\hat x^- + P^-z}{P^- + R} = \hat x^- + \frac{P^-}{P^- + R}\,(z - \hat x^-)$$

— the Kalman update with $K = P^-/(P^- + R)$. The curvature $J'' = 1/P^- + 1/R$ is the information of the estimate, so $P^+ = 1/J''$:

$$\frac{1}{P^+} = \frac{1}{P^-} + \frac{1}{R}, \qquad P^+ = \frac{P^-R}{P^- + R} = (1 - K)P^-$$
